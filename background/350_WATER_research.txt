TERRITORY AND OWNERSHIP

WATER commits installed water-service capability and its physical operation: pressurized conveyance, pumping, service storage, hydraulic districting, valve configurations, network residual disinfectant capability, supply calendars, live equipment actions and hydraulic/quality recovery. Bulk transmission and local distribution belong together when the promised output is pressure/quality-feasible utility service.
Installed wastewater-transfer pumping is included where the contract is pumping, wet-well storage and hydraulic service. Wastewater biological/chemical treatment recipes, treatment-train synthesis, membranes, filter backwash and process regeneration belong to PROC when transformation feasibility defines the output. Sewer civil works, drainage asset construction and maintenance belong to CIVIC. Basin-scale flood, catchment, irrigation-right and river-allocation decisions belong to LAND. A water utility's corporate organization does not override these contract boundaries.
Withdrawal permits, well activation and depletion belong to EXTR or LAND according to the resource contract. Supplied source inflows, permitted withdrawals and treatment-output qualities are boundary data for WATER.
Pipe capacity and connection configuration remain WATER when hydraulic service capability is delivered. Choosing which deteriorated assets to renew and when crews excavate them is CIVIC. The same replacement can appear in two coupled models with different delivered commitments; do not merge their contracts.
Districting is admitted only when it commits a hydraulic service partition and its boundary connections. Observation-only meter/sensor placement and leak localization are COMP interfaces. Adversarial protection and attack-response policies are INTEL. Restoring pressure or clearing accidental quality impairment from a specified observed network state remains WATER.
Water tanker journeys belong to LOG. Spare-parts and treatment-chemical replenishment belong to SCM. Standalone operating/repair rosters belong to HR. Tariffs and commercial rights belong to MKT; financing belongs to FIN.
Continuous flows, pressures, chlorine doses and tank levels may accompany an ICO decision. Their numerical discretization, nonlinear equations or mixed-integer approximation do not establish a separate combinatorial Problem.

CANDIDATE INVENTORY

PIPE — Water Network Configuration and Pipe Sizing
Decision: choose permitted pipe connections and assign catalogue sizes to proposed or upgraded pipe reaches. Deliver a hydraulic network configuration that meets service demand and pressure constraints within investment limits.
Aliases: least-cost network design, discrete pipe sizing, hydraulic network reinforcement. Fixed-topology diameter choice is a specialization; topology additions, parallel pipes and hydraulic upsizing are supported variants. Ordinary condition-based replacement prioritization is excluded.
Construction audit: existing geography, candidate corridors and demand points are supplied. The solver assigns sizes (#map) and constructs optional connections (#link). Flow direction and head loss are physical consequences; they do not independently establish Structure.
Candidate classification: #design; #map + #link; #strategic, ##network-design, ##pipe-sizing. Priority Critical because conveyance capability constrains the core service; Relevance Critical because capacity persists for long asset lives; Hardness High because discrete options interact with nonconvex hydraulic feasibility.
IE: hydraulic network capacity design and pressure verification. OR: exact mixed-integer convex design for its supported fixed-resistance model; MINLP and hydraulic simulation with evolutionary search for extensions (S01-S03, S08).
Admission: retain. Separate from PUMPSTORE because pipe diameter/connectivity and installed pump/storage capacity can be procured and optimized independently. Integrated design remains an allowed coupling, not another parent.
Uncertainty: the exact formulation in S02 must not be generalized to arbitrary pumps, water quality or investment staging.

PUMPSTORE — Pumping and Service-Storage Configuration
Decision: select pump configurations, pump counts, tank sites and discrete capacity options for an installed utility system. Continuous size refinement can accompany the discrete choices.
Aliases: pump-station configuration, tank siting and sizing, service-reservoir design. Emergency storage and redundant pump options are variants. Basin reservoirs and new withdrawal-resource portfolios are not.
Supplied: pipe network, terrain, service envelope and equipment catalogues. Chosen: equipment/capacity options (#select) and physical tank/equipment sites (#place). Pump curves and tank balances are constitutive conditions.
Classification: #design; #select + #place; #strategic, ##pump-station, ##storage. Priority High: pumping and storage recur widely but gravity-only systems exist. Relevance Critical: capacity and redundancy set long-lived service and energy limits. Hardness High: designs must work across operating cycles, demand patterns and nonlinear pump characteristics.
IE: pumping-station and service-storage sizing under extended-period hydraulics. OR: hydraulic simulation with genetic configuration search and continuous sizing refinement (S03, S04).
Admission: retain; co-design with PIPE is material. Pure continuous tank sizing without a siting, count or catalogue choice is outside the ICO subset. Wastewater wet-well/pump configuration is a hydraulic variant where the same contract holds; do not claim that potable-water design benchmarks validate every wastewater detail.

DISTRICT — District Metered Area Design
Decision: partition a supplied water network into service districts and choose which boundary links are closed or metered/open. Deliver a hydraulically feasible district structure.
Aliases: water network partitioning, DMA design, sectorization. Pressure-zone and dynamic-DMA design are variants when the output remains a district service configuration.
Supplied: graph, demand, elevations, available boundary equipment. Chosen: node-to-district membership (#map) and interdistrict connection arrangement (#link). Mere clustering of measurement data is not this contract.
Classification: #design; #map + #link; #tactical, ##partition, ##dma. Priority High: common in leakage-managed distribution systems. Relevance High: district organization affects leakage management, service pressure and operability. Hardness High: graph partitions must satisfy hydraulic and water-quality conditions.
IE: hydraulic districting and boundary-meter design. OR: graph partitioning with hydraulic simulation and multiobjective search; integrated valve-setting optimization where required (S05, S06).
Admission: retain. This is not observation-only meter placement. Temporary opening of existing boundaries is an operational variant under SCHEDULE or DISPATCH, not a new design problem.

ISOLATION — Isolation Valve Placement
Decision: choose isolation-valve sites to limit service loss when failed pipe segments must be disconnected.
Aliases: isolation-valve system design, segmentation for burst isolation. Adding valves to an installed network and complete redesign are variants; valve inspection and replacement-work calendars belong to CIVIC.
Supplied: pipe layout and failure scenarios. Chosen: physical valve positions (#place) and selected installation sites (#select). The segment graph is induced by the design; it is not automatically a chosen link construction.
Classification: #design; #place + #select; ##isolation, ##resilience. Priority High because segment isolation is a recurring requirement. Relevance High because placement changes the number of affected consumers. Hardness High because many site combinations and failure consequences must be evaluated.
IE: segment isolation and demand-shortfall design. OR: genetic layout search with segment or hydraulic evaluation; coupled pipe/valve optimization is a material alternative (S07, S08).
Admission: retain. DISTRICT delivers normal hydraulic service partitions; ISOLATION delivers failure-isolation capability. A shutoff valve can participate in both, but the decision contracts remain distinct.
Limit: graph vulnerability proxies are screening tools, not hydraulic service guarantees.

PRESSURE — Pressure-Control Valve Placement
Decision: choose pressure-regulating device sites and design settings while maintaining required downstream service pressure.
Aliases: PRV placement, pressure-management design, leakage-control valve siting. New installations and retrofits are variants. Continuous settings of fixed devices alone are excluded from ICO admission.
Supplied: hydraulic network and demand scenarios. Chosen: installed device locations (#place) and participating installations (#select). Leakage and head constraints evaluate the result.
Classification: #design; #place + #select; ##pressure-management, ##leakage. Priority High: pressure management is material across many distribution systems. Relevance High: settings and locations affect leakage and service. Hardness High: placement interacts with nonlinear hydraulics.
IE: pressure-management zone and PRV design. OR: placement MINLP with convex relaxation/rounding; hydraulic genetic search with embedded setting optimization (S06, S09, S10).
Admission: retain. It may be integrated with DISTRICT or BOOSTER; independent pressure-capability commitments remain identifiable. Energy-recovery devices can be a variant when water-service pressure management dominates; a separate electricity-generation commitment needs ENER contract review.

BOOSTER — Disinfectant Booster Placement
Decision: select network booster stations and their locations to maintain distribution-system disinfectant residuals under dose and quality limits.
Aliases: booster chlorination design, booster station siting. Species/byproduct models and joint valve siting are variants; standalone treatment chemistry or treatment-plant flowsheet synthesis are PROC.
Supplied: candidate network nodes, hydraulic transport and quality requirements. Chosen: booster sites (#place) and installations (#select). Doses and reaction/transport dynamics determine feasibility.
Classification: #design; #place + #select; ##booster-chlorination, ##water-quality. Priority Medium: relevant to networks that need distributed rechlorination. Relevance High: location determines residual-control capability. Hardness High: site choices couple hydraulic transport, decay and dosing.
IE: distribution residual-management design. OR: siting MINLP and convex-relaxation heuristics, with hydraulic/quality simulation (S09-S11).
Admission: retain as utility network quality capability. It is not a new treatment-process design. Continuous dosing at installed stations remains an accompanying control decision, not another ICO card.

SCHEDULE — Pump and Valve Scheduling
Decision: schedule installed pump starts/stops and controllable valve states with service storage. Meet water demand or wastewater inflow requirements, pressure/capacity limits, permitted switching and terminal storage conditions.
Aliases: optimal pump scheduling, optimal water flow with discrete controls, energy-aware pumping. Fixed/variable-speed pumps, tariff response, joint tank-level planning, operational source switching, dynamic boundary valves and wet-well pumping are variants.
Supplied: equipment and network, demand/inflow forecasts, source limits and electricity conditions. Chosen: operating intervals (#place) and active equipment/modes (#select). Water levels, pressures and continuous speeds are state/control variables, not separate structures.
Classification: #plan; #place + #select; #rolling, ##pump-scheduling, ##storage. Priority Critical: the recurring core operating calendar. Relevance High: energy and service feasibility recur daily. Hardness High: nonlinear network equations, storage trajectories and switches require formulation engineering.
IE: forecast-based pumping and storage scheduling. OR: MINLP; strengthened MILP/polyhedral approximation with nonlinear feasibility validation; simulation-based search as a material alternative (S12-S14, S18, S19).
Admission: retain. Distinguish an issued future equipment calendar from DISPATCH's immediate action under observed state. Rolling recomputation alone does not create a new Problem.
Sewer limit: only real pump/gate choices qualify. Overflow-indicator or piecewise hydraulic binaries alone do not qualify (S20, S21).

ROTATION — Intermittent Water Supply Scheduling
Decision: choose service windows and rotations for zones when available hydraulic supply cannot provide continuous service. Respect pressure, available quantity and service-equity conditions.
Aliases: rotational supply, rationing schedule, intermittent distribution scheduling. Rural/urban systems and planned shortage regimes are variants.
Supplied: districts, admissible valve actions, water availability and service requirements. Chosen: service windows (#place) and zones supplied in each window (#select). Basin allocation and rights setting remain outside WATER.
Classification: #plan; #place + #select; ##intermittent-supply, ##equity. Priority Medium: restricted to shortage or intermittent systems. Relevance Critical: choices can materially change access to essential water. Hardness High for hydraulically coupled systems with equity and network filling; simpler fixed-capacity ILP models can be easier.
IE: zone service rotation and equitable delivery scheduling. OR: integer supply scheduling with hydraulic constraints or simulation evaluation (S15, S16).
Admission: retain. Unlike SCHEDULE, it commits consumer service availability; pump scheduling implements the accepted calendar. Integrated models may solve both.
No separate #allocate card for continuous zone flow quantities; discreteness must come from named service windows or admitted discrete service blocks.

DISPATCH — Feedback Pump and Valve Dispatch
Decision: choose the next pump/valve activation actions from observed tank levels, demand/inflow and equipment availability while protecting short-cycle hydraulic service.
Aliases: closed-loop pumping control, operational water-network MPC with discrete actuation. Potable supply and wastewater transfer are variants where actual equipment-state choices remain.
Supplied: installed assets, current state and any issued schedule. Chosen: active units and valve actions (#select); continuous settings are coordinated controls. A supplied pipe graph does not add #link by itself.
Classification: #dispatch; #select; #online, #closedloop, #rolling, ##predictive-control. Priority High: feedback action selection recurs in automated systems. Relevance High: it maintains service as actual conditions depart from forecasts. Hardness High: latency, forecast error and nonlinear feasibility restrict search.
IE: state-feedback pumping control. OR: mixed-integer predictive control or simulation-based mixed discrete/continuous search (S17-S19, S31, S32). Clock/tank-level rules remain operational comparators.
Admission: retain. Admit wastewater operation only with physical pump/gate activation choices. Exclude continuous-setpoint models whose binaries only represent overflow or weir physics (S20, S33). No separate sewer-MPC parent is admitted.

FAILURE — Hydraulic Failure Isolation and Service Recovery
Decision: revise the current network operating configuration after a burst or equipment outage; isolate damage and select valve, pump and available bypass interventions that restore feasible service.
Aliases: burst response, network failure recovery, service restoration. Power loss and treatment-works outages are external failure causes when the response commits network operation.
Supplied: failure location, current state, remaining equipment and feasible intervention candidates. Chosen: intervention set (#select) and temporary service connections where allowed (#link). Excavation, repair tasks and work crews are not the output.
Classification: #adapt; #select + #link; #online, ##service-restoration. Priority High: failures recurrently invalidate service commitments. Relevance Critical: response limits large service interruptions. Hardness High: disrupted hydraulics and intervention combinations require screened or heuristic search.
IE: hydraulic isolation and service-recovery response. OR: multiobjective hydraulic simulation-optimization; screened intervention heuristics (S22, S23).
Admission: retain. Temporary bypass selection is included, but independent construction/repair execution is CIVIC (S29). No universal real-time completion claim.
The result changes the network state used by subsequent schedules and dispatch.

QUALITY — Water-Quality Incident Flushing
Decision: select and time hydrant openings and valve operations to remove a specified quality impairment while respecting hydraulic service and intervention limits.
Aliases: contaminant flushing response, quality-incident hydraulic recovery. Accidental contamination and off-specification water are in scope; a strategic attacker/defender contract is not.
Supplied: observed or scenario-estimated impairment, existing network and device availability. Chosen: active flushing/isolation actions (#select) and activation times (#place); transport and mixing are feasibility/evaluation dynamics.
Classification: #adapt; #select + #place; #online, ##flushing, ##water-quality. Priority Medium: an incident-specific operating mode. Relevance Critical: response protects essential delivered water quality. Hardness High: hydraulic-quality simulation, possible source uncertainty and action order interact.
IE: quality-incident isolation and flushing response. OR: hydraulic-quality simulation with multiobjective genetic search; mixed-integer device scheduling where activation timing is explicit (S24-S26).
Admission: retain. Routine pipe-cleaning campaigns in S27 deliver asset-maintenance work and are transferred to CIVIC. The shared flushing technique does not collapse that contract into incident-driven quality recovery. Medical/public-health decisions remain outside WATER.
Separate from FAILURE because the restored artifact must satisfy transported-quality criteria, not only pressure and delivered quantity.

Within #plan, ROTATION precedes SCHEDULE if both occur. Within #design, PIPE precedes dependent configurations; DISTRICT precedes PRESSURE. Remaining ties follow assets, segmentation, control, then quality capability. Within #adapt, hydraulic failure recovery precedes quality recovery as a stable presentation tie; neither must cause the other.

ALIASES, EXCLUSIONS AND COVERAGE DISPOSITIONS

- Pure flow, tank-level, blending and chlorine-dose optimization: outside the combinatorial subset. Source-water mixing evidence S30 does not justify a separate discrete source-allocation parent.
- Optimal water flow: SCHEDULE or DISPATCH only when the contract includes actual discrete controls; otherwise continuous optimization.
- Dynamic DMA operation: merge scheduled boundary-valve choices into SCHEDULE and immediate state choices into DISPATCH. S28 studies continuous settings, so it cannot alone support another ICO parent.
- Leakage minimization: objective of PRESSURE, DISTRICT or operating controls. Leak detection and sensor placement are distinct observation contracts owned by COMP.
- General sewer MPC: retain only actual activation decisions in SCHEDULE/DISPATCH. Switching overflow regimes in S20 are model physics, not automatically chosen operations.
- Routine unidirectional flushing: transfer the periodic asset-cleaning campaign to CIVIC. S27 delivers a sequence of cleaning actions with staff-effort objectives. Incident-driven hydraulic quality recovery remains QUALITY; classification follows the delivered contract.
- Basin rights, catchment allocation and irrigation entitlement: LAND. Installed distribution feasibility does not transfer resource ownership to WATER.
- Well/prospect selection and extraction/depletion: EXTR. Existing permitted source output can feed WATER.
- Pipe rehabilitation portfolios, maintenance calendars, repair sequencing and construction work: CIVIC; S29 provides a repair-scheduling template. WATER can receive outage windows and return hydraulic effects.
- Chemical/biological treatment, desalination-process selection and filter/regeneration schedules: PROC when process state defines feasibility. Finished treated-water availability can feed WATER.
- Standalone sensor/meter placement and inspection-data collection: COMP unless protective/adversarial conditions define an INTEL contract.
- Standalone labor rosters: HR. Tanker delivery: LOG. Stock replenishment: SCM. Commercial allocation: MKT. Financing: FIN.
- Fire-flow capacity: hydraulic stress scenario in PIPE/PUMPSTORE, not a new parent.
- Electricity tariffs, carbon prices and flexibility provision: objectives or constraints in SCHEDULE. A separate electricity-market offer is not this water-service contract.
- Water reuse: network hydraulic distribution fits existing candidates; contaminant treatment/reuse-process synthesis is PROC. No new Industry Domain is created.

SEARCH COVERAGE AND LIMITS

Pass 1 covered hydraulic supply design, pump/tank operation, intermittent supply and wastewater-transfer control. It established genuine discrete actions and highlighted continuous-only models.
Pass 2 covered valve siting, hydraulic districting, booster chlorination, failure isolation, recovery and flushing. It added distinct capability and incident-response contracts.
Pass 3 tested omissions through source blending, planned maintenance, dynamic topology, sewer MPC, treatment interfaces and energy response. Findings mostly mapped to existing contracts, continuous subcases or neighboring owners.
Pass 4 checked stronger counterevidence, including separately retrieved author material S32-S33: S28 continuous valve optimization cannot prove a separate discrete topology parent; S20 hybrid sewer equations cannot prove selectable physical modes; S29 repair schedules are not WATER hydraulic recovery.
Practical coverage is broad at the selected contract granularity; no claim of globally exhaustive discovery or universal SOTA is made. The largest evidence limitation is full-text accessibility for several publisher sources. The sources still establish the stated decisions and Template families; specific industrial-scale comparisons, licenses and reproducible baselines require later case research.
No dataset, executable benchmark, performance uplift or guarantee is promoted to a canonical Case. Specialized reclaimed-water configurations, emergency blending and integrated drainage/service models need a fresh contract test if later evidence exposes a distinct decision.

CASE RESEARCH — WATER302 PUMP AND VALVE SCHEDULING
10 October 2026 | Second calibration Case | INDUSTRY first

PARENT
WATER302 Pump and Valve Scheduling.
The parent Problem is fixed.

INDUSTRY CASE — WATER302c

CASE
Schedule installed pump starts, stops, speeds and material valve states over a forecast horizon while maintaining storage and hydraulic service constraints.

SOTA
The strongest defensible current industry state combines a calibrated hydraulic digital twin with current operating state, short-horizon demand/source/energy forecasts, mathematical or simulation-based optimization, and hydraulic look-ahead validation.
TwinOptPRO is a current exemplar: a 24-hour pump-control schedule is computed from forecasts plus current tank and valve state; it specifies transport flows and individual pump speeds or on/off states and is checked with a 24-hour look-ahead simulation [W01, W02].
Commercial platforms also expose pump scheduling, SCADA integration and digital-twin workflows [W06, W07].
Current research extends objectives beyond energy cost to hydraulic safety and equipment life and still uses multi-objective/metaheuristic or predictive-control architectures where nonlinear hydraulic fidelity limits monolithic exact optimization [W03, W15].
No single universal implementation is established as dominant.

CONTEXT
Drinking-water production, transmission and distribution systems with multiple pump stations, tanks and controllable valves.
Material conditions include nonlinear head-flow relations, tank trajectories, fixed-speed and variable-speed pumps, electricity prices or renewable availability, switching restrictions, terminal storage targets and forecast uncertainty.
Issued day-ahead or other forecast-horizon equipment calendars belong here. Immediate feedback actions remain WATER801.

GAP
Public pump-scheduling benchmarks exist, but canonical sets are largely inherited from older academic networks such as Van Zyl, Richmond and Anytown [W04, W05].
They remain useful for algorithm comparison but do not jointly represent current industrial conditions: multi-station fixed/VFD equipment, current pump efficiency or degradation, material valve actions, live state initialization, forecast uncertainty, dynamic energy/renewable signals, switch wear, terminal-state requirements and multi-objective safety conditions.
Reported utility and research gains are normally measured against conventional operation, local rules or a previous controller rather than against the strongest current SOTA on a common industrial-scale dataset [W03, W08, W09, W15].
Proposed uplift direction: build a purpose-designed multi-regime WATER302 benchmark preserving nonlinear hydraulic feasibility and current operating realism, then test whether v10 structural decomposition or move design produces repeatable cost/energy/service uplift over strong hierarchical, MILP/relaxation, predictive-control and simulation-optimization baselines.

ATTEMPTS / CURRENT METHOD FAMILIES
- Nonlinear or mixed-integer pump scheduling with hydraulic equations.
- MILP/polyhedral or surrogate approximations followed by nonlinear hydraulic validation.
- Hierarchical decomposition between network/tank planning and individual pump-group operation.
- Simulation-based evolutionary and other metaheuristic scheduling.
- Rolling or predictive-control schedules using updated forecasts and current state.
- Digital-twin scheduling combining SCADA, forecasts, hydraulic models and optimization.
- Pump-level efficiency optimization as an adjacent WATER801 comparator.

BENCHMARK SUPPORT
The public benchmark base is stronger than in AUTOM703 but still inadequate for a current industrial SOTA claim.
Van Zyl and Richmond remain established public pump-scheduling references [W04, W05].
The public Richmond skeleton is explicitly schematic rather than hydraulically equivalent to the real system [W05].
Recent public EPANET-derived state datasets can support stress construction but are not themselves validated day-ahead pump-scheduling benchmark suites [W16].
TwinOptPRO supplies a current industrial architecture and pilot setting, but its utility data are not a general public benchmark [W01, W02].

PURPOSE-BUILT BENCHMARK BRIEF — WATER302

CLAIM TO TEST
Can v10 improve on strong modern day-ahead pump/valve scheduling while preserving nonlinear hydraulic feasibility and operationally credible equipment use?

CORE INSTANCE DATA
- hydraulic network topology and parameters;
- multiple pump stations with calibrated pump curves;
- fixed-speed and variable-speed equipment where applicable;
- tanks and initial storage levels;
- material controllable valves;
- demand/source forecasts and realizations;
- time-varying electricity tariff and, where relevant, renewable availability;
- switching limits or minimum run/off restrictions where used;
- terminal storage or cyclic-balance requirements;
- objective terms needed to reproduce each baseline.

REGIME AXES
Vary network and pump-station scale, storage flexibility, forecast error, tariff/renewable volatility, hydraulic tightness, pump/valve discreteness, switching limits, efficiency-curve heterogeneity and terminal-state tightness.

OPTIONAL EXTENSIONS
Add equipment degradation, transient-pressure penalty, outage scenarios or water-quality restrictions only when they preserve WATER302 rather than silently changing the Problem.

BASELINES
At minimum:
- strong MILP/polyhedral or other tractable approximation with hydraulic validation;
- hierarchical current architecture comparable to TwinOptPRO where reconstructible;
- strong simulation-optimization/metaheuristic baseline;
- rolling/predictive-control baseline when forecast updates are part of the instance.
Legacy trigger-level or manual schedules are operational comparators, not sufficient SOTA baselines.

CONSTRUCTION AND VALIDATION
Prefer anonymized partner-utility models where available.
Otherwise extend public hydraulic models with explicitly reconstructed modern pump, valve, tariff and uncertainty layers.
Validate hydraulic, storage, switch-count, energy and objective distributions against current utility evidence.
Keep generation code, hydraulic-engine version, units and baseline implementations reproducible.
Use held-out instances and hidden parameter draws for final uplift claims.

DELIVERABLE
A purpose-built WATER302 benchmark can itself be a service artifact: public core benchmark where licensing permits, private calibrated utility extension, baseline suite, validation harness and an explicit map from benchmark conditions to client operating constraints.
